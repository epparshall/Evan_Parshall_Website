I recently gave a talk at my company, SEP, about the research behind why we sleep and how it impacts our performance as engineers. This post is an adaptation of that talk. You can also find the original version on the [SEP Blog](https://sep.com/blog/sleep-on-it-the-engineering-case-for-better-rest/).

A few months ago I read a book called *Why We Sleep* by Matthew Walker, a neuroscientist who runs the Center for Human Sleep Science at UC Berkeley. As an engineer, I wanted to look at the actual research behind his claims. I found that much of it is backed by solid, well-replicated science.

Most people know sleep is important, but understanding the *mechanism* behind it makes you more likely to change your behavior. The quality of our code, our design decisions, and our health are all impacted by sleep.

## Why Do We Sleep At All?
We spend roughly a third of our lives unconscious. From an evolutionary perspective, this is a massive liability—you can’t eat, work, or defend yourself. Yet, every animal studied sleeps in some form. This tells us sleep is not just helpful; it is essential. Nature decided the risk of being unconscious was worth the critical biological work happening during those hours.

## Why We Get Tired: The Two Systems
Two systems determine how tired you feel:

1. **Circadian Rhythm:** An internal 24-hour clock. It persists even without sunlight, though natural light keeps it synchronized with the world.
2. **Sleep Pressure (Adenosine):** A chemical that builds up in your brain the longer you are awake. Sleep clears this "weight."

Tiredness is the gap between these two: high sleep pressure minus a low circadian wake drive equals extreme sleepiness.

### The Caffeine Trap
Caffeine doesn't give you energy; it blocks adenosine receptors. It "parks in the spot" so the tired signal can't get through. However, adenosine keeps building up behind the block. When the caffeine wears off (it has a half-life of 5–7 hours), all that built-up adenosine floods back, causing the "caffeine crash."

## What Actually Happens When You Sleep
Sleep is a structured process consisting of 90-minute cycles with four stages:

* **N1 & N2:** Light sleep; the doorway to deeper stages.
* **N3 (Deep Sleep):** The "nightly save operation." It moves memories from the hippocampus (RAM) to the cortex (SSD). It’s also when growth hormones are released for physical repair.
* **REM (Rapid Eye Movement):** The stage for dreaming, emotional processing, and creative problem-solving.

**Crucial Note:** Deep sleep dominates the first half of the night, while REM dominates the second half. If you cut 8 hours of sleep down to 6, you aren't just losing 25% of your sleep—you are losing the majority of your REM sleep.

## What Sleep Deprivation Does to the Brain
* **Cognitive Decline:** After two weeks of 6-hour nights, performance drops to the level of someone who hasn't slept for two days.
* **Subjective Blindness:** Sleep-deprived people lose the ability to accurately judge their own impairment. They feel "fine" while their objective performance continues to tank.
* **Emotional Volatility:** The amygdala (emotional alarm) becomes 60% more reactive, while the prefrontal cortex (the "brake") weakens. This leads to impulsive decisions and misreading neutral faces as hostile.
* **Long-term Risk:** Sleep flushes out amyloid beta, a protein linked to Alzheimer’s. Consistently sleeping 6 hours or less in middle age is linked to a 30% increased risk of dementia.

## What Sleep Deprivation Does to the Body
* **Immune System:** One night of 4-hour sleep reduces natural killer cell activity by 70%.
* **Heart Health:** During the spring "spring forward" (losing one hour of sleep), there is a 24% jump in heart attacks the following Monday.
* **Metabolism:** Sleep loss increases Ghrelin (hunger hormone) and decreases Leptin (fullness hormone), leading to overeating and reduced insulin sensitivity.

## REM and Dreams: The Nightly Therapy
REM sleep acts as "overnight therapy." It replays emotional memories in a brain environment free of noradrenaline (a stress chemical). This allows us to "remember the lesson but lose the sting." 

REM also fosters creativity. It relaxes logical rules to find connections between distant ideas. A study showed that people who slept were twice as likely to find a "hidden shortcut" in a math problem compared to those who stayed awake.

## What You Can Actually Do About It
1. **Stick to a Schedule:** Wake up at the same time every day, even on weekends.
2. **Morning Sunlight:** Get 20–30 minutes of natural light early to anchor your circadian clock.
3. **Temperature Control:** Your body needs to drop its core temperature by 1°C to sleep. A hot shower before bed or wearing socks can help by dilating blood vessels to dump core heat.
4. **Caffeine Cutoff:** Stop caffeine by noon or 2 PM.
5. **Alcohol Awareness:** Alcohol is a sedative, not a sleep aid. It suppresses REM and causes "micro-awakenings."
6. **The 20-Minute Rule:** If you can't sleep after 20 minutes, get out of bed. Do something boring in dim light until you feel sleepy to avoid associating the bed with anxiety.
7. **Screen Hygiene:** Use "Night Shift" modes to filter blue light, which suppresses melatonin.

## Closing
Prioritizing sleep isn't self-indulgent; it's a foundational engineering requirement for a high-performing brain and body. I recently asked ChatGPT for the single most important thing to maintain productivity, and the answer was: "consistent 8 hours of sleep."
