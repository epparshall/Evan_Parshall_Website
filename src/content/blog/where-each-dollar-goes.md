A reader who finished the [first post](/blog/money-is-freedom) in this series knows why it is important to save: money is what allows you to spend your time on what matters most to you. This second post is the more practical question that follows. You have a paycheck. Some of it has to cover living expenses like rent and groceries. What is left has to go somewhere. Where?

This post is the answer. It is the order I follow when I get paid, and it is a similar order most personal finance writers converge on for good reasons. I will explain why the order is what it is, what each step is actually doing for you, and where the common traps are.

A note before I start. You may already have some of these steps covered. The order is meant to be sequential, but you only need to read the steps you have not yet covered, although reading everything certainly would not hurt.

One more note, on the numbers. Every contribution limit and dollar threshold in this post is for the **2026 tax year**, and the IRS adjusts most of them every year; the current figures are always on the IRS pages for [401(k) limits](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-401k-and-profit-sharing-plan-contribution-limits), [IRA limits](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-ira-contribution-limits), and [HSA limits](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p969). The interest rates I quote move even faster. If you are reading this long after publication, the order of the steps should hold up fine, but check the current numbers before acting on them, and asking a capable LLM what has changed since 2026 is a quick way to do exactly that.

## The Order

1. Capture your employer match (401(k), HSA, wellness incentives)
2. Build an emergency fund right-sized for your life
3. Pay off any high-interest debt (anything above roughly 6 or 7%)
4. Max out your Roth IRA
5. Contribute to your HSA beyond the match, ideally maxing it if you can contribute through payroll
6. Split the rest between additional retirement-account contributions and a taxable brokerage

The order matches the expected return on each dollar. Each step roughly earns you more than the one after it. People who skip steps usually do so because they are excited about investing and want to put money in the market before they have built the right foundation. That excitement costs them.

## Step 1: Capture Your Employer Match

The highest-return investment available to you is the match your employer offers. The most common is the 401(k) match, but many employers also match HSA contributions, offer wellness incentives, or have other matching programs. Always take the 401(k) and HSA matches. There is no situation where it makes sense to leave those on the table; they are pure free money. The matches I would think twice about are ones that require you to spend money you would not otherwise spend, or buy something you do not need, just to capture them.

If your employer offers a 401(k) match, the math is incredible. Most companies offer something like a full match on the first 3 to 6% of your salary you contribute. If you make $80,000 and your employer matches the first 5% dollar for dollar, you contribute $4,000 and they give you another $4,000. That is a 100% return before the money has even been invested.

Ask your HR department what the exact match formula is. Match formulas vary: dollar for dollar, fifty cents on the dollar, capped at different percentages, sometimes with a vesting schedule.

A word on vesting. Vesting is the schedule by which your employer's match becomes legally yours. You always own your own contributions immediately. The match is different. If you leave the company before the vesting period ends, you forfeit some or all of the match. Important: the match is still invested the whole time, so you see the balance and it compounds, even though part of it is not legally yours until you are fully vested. Common vesting schedules:

- **Cliff vesting:** 0% until you hit the cliff (often 3 years), then 100% at once. Leave before the cliff and you lose all of it.
- **Graded vesting:** a percentage vests each year. Common version is 20% per year over five years.
- **Immediate vesting:** rare but generous. The match is yours the moment it hits your account.

In my experience, most companies fully vest you around the five-year mark. Vesting matters for timing. If you are planning to leave, the difference of a few weeks or months can be the difference between walking away with all of the match or none of it. Do not quit two weeks before a vesting cliff or the next vesting grade if you can help it. Check your schedule before you give notice.

Get the HSA match too, if you have one. Some employers will also deposit money into your HSA if you complete annual preventive care (physical, blood test, dental visits). Some match HSA contributions the way they match 401(k). Either way, this is free money on the same logic as the 401(k) match. Always take it.

If your employer offers an FSA match, it can be very useful, but contribute carefully. FSAs are mostly use-it-or-lose-it: you can only roll over a small amount (around $680 in 2026) to the following year, and anything above that disappears.[^1] So use the match to fund up to what you actually expect to spend plus what you can roll over, and no more, so you do not forfeit money at year end.

A quick way to figure out all of this: pull up your benefits PDF from your HR portal and feed it to an LLM (Claude, ChatGPT, whatever you use). Ask it to summarize your 401(k) match, vesting schedule, HSA contributions, wellness incentives, and any other free money. The same approach works for selecting benefits at open enrollment. Most people spend two hours flipping through PDFs trying to compare plans. An LLM does it in two minutes, and if it already knows your situation, it can tell you which plan actually fits you.

People skip employer matches for two reasons. Either they do not realize the match exists, or they think they cannot afford the contribution because money is tight. I would strongly push back on the second. If money is tight, free money is exactly what you need. (Hypothetically, if you really needed the cash, you could even take the match, withdraw the money immediately, eat the early-withdrawal penalty plus income tax, and you would still come out way ahead. Every matched dollar, even after the 32% combined tax-and-penalty hit, is still 68 cents of free money. This author does not recommend doing this, but the math makes the point about how good a match is.)

The full picture for when money is genuinely tight is covered [later in this post](#when-money-is-tight-sequencing-the-first-three-steps) once all the pieces are defined.

## Step 2: An Emergency Fund Right-Sized for Your Life

Before you do anything else, you need cash on hand for the surprises life will throw at you. How much depends on your life.

The way I think about this is not "how many months of expenses should I save" but "what is the minimum cash I need to be confident I will not have to liquidate investments or take on debt in a bind." That number is highly personal. If you have a stable job, a partner who also earns, low fixed expenses, and family who could front you money in a real emergency, your number is lower. If you are the only earner, you have kids, your job is volatile, or you have no family backstop, your number is higher.

For my own situation, I keep less in cash than a lot of personal finance writers would recommend. I have a stable job and generous parents I could ask for money in an extreme situation. I have never had to, and I would prefer not to, but the existence of that backstop means I can keep more of my money invested. Everyone's situation is different, so adjust accordingly.

The standard guidance is three to six months of essential expenses. "Essential" is the lean version of your budget: rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, minimum debt payments, basic transportation. Not the gym membership you would cancel. Not the streaming services. Not the annual vacation. The number you would actually need to keep yourself housed and fed for a few months if the income stopped.

Where to park it is important. Standard checking accounts at the big banks (Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo) and most credit unions pay around 0.01%. That is the wrong place for an emergency fund. You want a high-yield savings account, a money market fund at a brokerage, or short-term Treasury bills. The current options worth knowing (as of this writing):

- **Ally and Marcus by Goldman Sachs:** well-regarded online banks paying around 4%.
- **Wealthfront and SoFi:** similar competitive rates, with broader fintech offerings.
- **Apple Savings:** 3.5% through Chase as of mid-2026, less competitive than it used to be when it was 4.5% via Goldman Sachs.
- **Fidelity Cash Management:** uses SPAXX by default, currently around 3.3%. Doubles as a checking account with a debit card.
- **SGOV:** an ETF that holds short-term Treasury bills. Pays about 4% and the interest is exempt from state and local income tax (more on this below).

Rates change. The right answer at any given time is whichever one is paying the best competitive rate. I personally use Fidelity. On a $20,000 emergency fund, 4% earns you $800 a year. At 0.01%, you earn $2.

One thing to flag on taxes. The interest you earn on these savings accounts is taxable income. For most readers, federal income tax is the bigger chunk (state and local are smaller for most states, zero for some). SGOV interest comes from Treasury bills, so it is exempt from state and local income tax, though still subject to federal. If you live in a high-tax state like California or New York, SGOV's state-tax exemption can be a meaningful boost.

A note on federally tax-exempt bond funds. ETFs like VTEB (Vanguard) and MUB (iShares) hold municipal bonds. The interest on these is exempt from federal income tax, which is the biggest of the three taxes for most people. The catch is that these are bond funds with real duration, so the price can move meaningfully when interest rates change. Think of it as a risk ladder: SGOV carries almost no price risk, a muni-bond fund like VTEB carries more, and a stock index fund like the S&P 500 carries the most. In this case, more risk leads to more expected reward. I would not put my full emergency fund in VTEB because of that price risk. But for money you are saving toward a goal that is a few years out, where you can ride out price swings, VTEB can make sense. A house down payment is the obvious example. So is money for someone a few years from retirement who wants something calmer than stocks but better-yielding than high-yield savings. Some people split: some of the emergency fund in high-yield savings, some in VTEB. Use caution.

The emergency fund is not an investment, and it is fine that it is not. Its job is to be there, untouched, when you need it. You are still putting it to work in a high-yield savings account or a Treasury fund, just safely. Resist the stronger temptation, which is to "really put it to work" by moving it into stocks. The whole point is that you can access it instantly without selling anything at a loss. If your goal is aggressive growth, that money belongs in a retirement or brokerage account, after you have an adequate emergency fund in place.

## Step 3: Kill High-Interest Debt

Now go after any debt with an interest rate above about 6 or 7%. Credit cards, private student loans, personal loans, and payday loans usually meet this bar. Payday loans should be priority one. I would never recommend taking one out in the first place, but if you have one, kill it before anything else.

Why 6 or 7% as the cutoff? Because that is roughly the long-term real return (real return meaning after inflation) of the stock market. If you can earn 7% investing or guarantee yourself 7% by paying off a 7% loan, the two are roughly equivalent. (Strictly, the loan payoff is a nominal return while the market figure is real, after inflation; treat the cutoff as a rule of thumb, not a precise line.) Anything above 7% and the math tips clearly toward paying off the debt. I would take a guaranteed 7% any day. 6% is on the edge, but if you are paying off a 6% loan to feel better, I would not blame you.

For high-interest debt, pay off the highest interest rate first. Always. Some personal finance writers recommend paying off the smallest balance first for the psychological win (the "snowball method"). I disagree. The math is unambiguous that highest rate first saves you more money, sometimes thousands of dollars more. Pay the highest rate first.

I covered the cost of carrying a credit card balance in the [first post](/blog/money-is-freedom#the-other-side-of-the-chessboard), so I will only recap the numbers here. A $5,000 balance at 21% paid at the minimum takes about 19 years to clear and costs you about $7,700 in interest.[^2][^3] Over those same 19 years, the $5,000 invested at 7% grows to about $18,000. The real cost of the balance is not the $7,700 in interest. It is the gap between owing $7,700 and owning $18,000, which is more than $25,000 of difference riding on a single $5,000 decision.

Once the high-interest debt is gone, this is a good moment to revisit your emergency fund. If you started with a smaller buffer to leave room for debt payments, you can add more now.

Understanding low-interest debt is just as important. Anything below 6 or 7% and the math tilts the other way. The classic example is a low-rate mortgage. If you have extra money and a 3.5% mortgage, putting it toward principal locks in a 3.5% return, while the same dollars in a brokerage have historically earned about 7%. The people who rushed to pay off 3% mortgages in 2020 chose to earn 3% when the market was paying more than double that.

There is a softer version of "paid off" worth naming. If you have enough in your brokerage to clear the mortgage tomorrow if you wanted to, then in every way that matters you are already free of it. The debt no longer has power over you, because you could end it at any moment. You are simply choosing to keep the money invested at a higher return instead. That is a stronger position than literally paying it off, because you keep the liquidity and the optionality while the interest works in your favor.

In truth, carrying any debt does have a psychological cost. However, paying off low-interest debt early is not recommended by this author. A friend of mine who is a financial advisor says her job is less about math and more about talking people off cliffs. Paying off low-interest debt early as an emotional choice is one of those cliffs.

Even on low-interest debt, still be sure to pay the minimum every month. Missing a minimum payment hits your credit score and triggers late fees. The strategy of not aggressively paying down low-rate debt is different from ignoring it. Turn on autopay, then invest what is left over.

## Step 4: Max Out Your Roth IRA

The Roth IRA is one of the best accounts in the U.S. tax code, and once your foundation is in place, filling it should be your next priority.

A few definitions first, since they come up constantly: pre-tax, post-tax, and tax-free growth.

**Pre-tax** money is income you have not yet paid income tax on. Your gross paycheck, the number before any taxes come out, is pre-tax. When you put pre-tax money into a traditional retirement account, none of it is taxed as income this year. It skips your income tax now. Later, when you withdraw it in retirement, the whole withdrawal is treated as income and you pay tax on it then.

Here is a pre-tax contribution in numbers, in the 22% tax bracket:

- You contribute $1,000 of gross (pre-tax) income to a traditional account.
- Because it skips income tax this year, your tax bill drops by $220. The contribution only "cost" you $780 in take-home pay.
- It grows at 7% for 30 years and becomes about $7,612.
- You withdraw it in retirement and pay 22% income tax on the whole thing: about $5,938 lands in your pocket.

**Post-tax** money is income you have already paid tax on. Your take-home paycheck is post-tax. When you put post-tax money into a Roth, you have already paid the tax going in, so you owe nothing on the way out. The number you see in the account is the number you own.

Here is a post-tax (Roth) contribution in numbers, same 22% bracket:

- You earn $1,000, pay $220 in tax, and contribute the remaining $780 to a Roth.
- It grows at 7% for 30 years and becomes about $5,938.
- You withdraw it in retirement and pay nothing: all $5,938 is yours.

Notice the two examples end in the exact same place: $5,938. That is the big secret of the pre-tax versus Roth debate. If your tax rate is the same going in and coming out, the two are mathematically identical. The decision only matters when your future tax rate differs from today's, which is what [Post 5](/blog/taxes-are-the-quiet-variable#roth-versus-traditional-fully-unpacked) digs into.

There is one tiebreaker, though, that quietly favors the Roth: it effectively lets you shelter more money. The annual contribution limit is the same dollar figure for both ($7,500 in 2026), but those dollars are not worth the same. $7,500 in a traditional account is pre-tax money you still owe tax on someday; at a 22% rate it is really worth about $5,850 to you in retirement. $7,500 in a Roth is already yours, free and clear. So by maxing the Roth, you fit more real, spendable retirement money under the same cap. When you can afford the larger up-front tax hit, that is a genuine edge.

**Tax-free growth** is the third piece, and it is the one people underrate. In a Roth, you never pay tax on the interest, dividends, or capital gains the money earns along the way. In a regular taxable brokerage, those gains get taxed.

Here is tax-free growth on a sale inside the account, in numbers:

- You bought a fund in your Roth for $10,000. It grows to $15,000.
- You sell it and buy a different fund, all inside the Roth. That is a $5,000 gain.
- In a taxable account, that sale triggers about $750 in long-term capital gains tax (15% of $5,000).
- In the Roth, you owe $0 in taxes.
- You can rebalance, switch funds, and take profits freely within the account, and the IRS never touches it as long as there is no early withdrawal from the account.

The same thing happens with income the holdings throw off along the way:

- A $10,000 fund pays a 2% dividend, so $200 lands in the account this year. In a taxable account you would owe about $30 on it (15% qualified rate). In the Roth, you keep all $200.
- A $10,000 bond position pays $500 of interest. In a taxable account that is taxed as ordinary income, about $110 gone at the 22% rate. In the Roth, you keep all $500.

And here is the same idea over a long horizon. A single $7,500 contribution growing at 7% for 30 years becomes about $57,000, a gain of roughly $49,500. In a Roth, that entire gain is untouched. In a taxable account, selling it would trigger roughly $7,400 in long-term capital gains tax. The Roth saves you that $7,400, and that is from one year's contribution. Do it every year for a career and the tax-free growth is the whole ballgame.

The Roth IRA is a retirement account where you contribute post-tax money, it grows tax-free for the rest of your life, and you withdraw it tax-free in retirement. No tax on the growth, and no tax on the qualified withdrawals. The 2026 contribution limit is $7,500 for people under 50 and $8,600 for those 50 and over.[^4] You have until April 15 of the following year to make contributions for the current tax year, which means you can make 2026 contributions all the way through April 15, 2027.

There is an income cap on direct Roth contributions. For 2026, the phase-out starts at $153,000 for single filers and $242,000 for married filing jointly.[^4] (The joint number is less than twice the single number, which is a marriage penalty for high earners. The tax code has a few of these.) Above those numbers you cannot contribute directly. There is a workaround called the backdoor Roth, which [Post 5](/blog/taxes-are-the-quiet-variable#the-backdoor-roth) covers. One thing to know now: contributing to a traditional IRA can complicate or hinder a backdoor Roth later because of the **pro-rata rule**. Pro rata means "proportionally": when a rule is pro rata, you do not get to choose which dollars you are touching. Any money you move is treated as a proportional blend of everything in the account, not just the slice you meant to move. [Post 5](/blog/taxes-are-the-quiet-variable#the-pro-rata-rule) covers how that complicates the backdoor Roth.

In numbers, because this idea shows up twice more in the series. Say an account holds $10,000: $8,000 of money that has already been taxed and $2,000 that has not. You want to move $1,000 out, and you would like for it to count as the already-taxed kind. Under a **pro-rata** rule, you do not get to pick: the account is 80% taxed money and 20% untaxed money, so your $1,000 is automatically treated as $800 of taxed money and $200 of untaxed money, and the $200 gets taxed on the way out. Under a **non-pro-rata** rule, like the Roth IRA's contributions-first ordering (which [Step 6 of this post](#step-6-retirement-accounts-beyond-the-match-and-taxable-brokerage) and [Post 5](/blog/taxes-are-the-quiet-variable#roth-versus-traditional-fully-unpacked) lean on), the already-taxed money is deemed to come out first, so your $1,000 comes out clean, and so would the next $7,000 after it.

The detailed Roth-vs-traditional question is also in [Post 5](/blog/taxes-are-the-quiet-variable#roth-versus-traditional-fully-unpacked). The short version: if you can afford to max the Roth without sacrificing other priorities, the Roth is hard to beat.

## Step 5: HSA Beyond the Match

You already captured your employer's HSA match in [Step 1](#step-1-capture-your-employer-match). Now the question is how much beyond that to contribute. You can only contribute to an HSA if you are on an HSA-eligible high-deductible health plan. If you are not on such a plan, you cannot use an HSA, but you may have access to an FSA instead; see [the appendix](#appendix-hsa-eligible-expenses-and-a-note-on-fsas) for how that works.

The HSA is the most tax-advantaged account in the U.S. system, and it is the only one with three tax breaks at once. Every dollar you contribute is deductible from your taxable income (so contributions are pre-tax). The money grows tax-free, and yes, you can invest it in stocks inside the HSA, or at minimum park it in a high-yield cash position rather than letting it sit idle. And withdrawals are tax-free if used for qualified medical expenses. The 2026 contribution limit is $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage. People 55 and over can contribute an extra $1,000.[^5]

The HSA has one major downside: the money is locked up. If you take it out for anything other than qualified medical expenses before 65, you pay income tax plus a 20% penalty.[^6] After 65, the HSA basically becomes a traditional IRA: you can withdraw for anything but you pay income tax on non-medical withdrawals. That is a remarkably good deal. The HSA goes from "medical-only account" to "regular retirement account" the moment you turn 65, with the bonus that medical withdrawals stay tax-free forever.

A quick word on choosing the high-deductible plan in the first place, since it is the gate to the HSA. If you are young and healthy, the high-deductible health plan paired with an HSA is usually the right choice. The premium is lower, the HSA gives you tax-advantaged savings, and even if you hit the deductible in a bad year, you generally come out ahead over a decade of relatively healthy years. I call this "betting on yourself." One distinction worth making: many employers offer several plans, and more than one may technically be a high-deductible, HSA-eligible plan. I deliberately pick the one with the highest deductible (and the lowest premium) of the eligible options, because it maximizes the premium savings I can redirect into the HSA and incentivizes me to stay healthy. Your situation might be different if you have a family, chronic conditions, or know you will need surgery. But for most healthy young people, the highest-deductible eligible plan is almost always the move.

Here is how I would think about contributions:

Contribute at least enough to cover your expected medical spending for the year. Every dollar you run through the HSA is a dollar you deducted from your taxable income, so you never pay income tax on it. For a reader in the 22 or 24% federal bracket, that is roughly a 22 to 24% discount on every prescription, copay, dental cleaning, eye exam, and emergency room visit. Worth doing even if you do not max the HSA.

If your employer lets you contribute to the HSA **through payroll**, those contributions skip more than just income tax. They also skip **FICA**, the 7.65% payroll tax that funds Social Security and Medicare.[^6] In most states, payroll contributions dodge state income tax too. No other account gets this treatment: your 401(k) contributions, traditional or Roth, still pay FICA. For someone in the 22% federal bracket, a payroll HSA contribution is saving roughly 30% in combined taxes on every dollar going in, before the money has grown a cent. [Post 5](/blog/taxes-are-the-quiet-variable#hsa-tax-mechanics-in-detail) walks through the exact math. (Note that the FICA savings are payroll-only. Money you deposit into the HSA directly still gets the income-tax deduction, but FICA was already withheld from your paycheck and you cannot get it back.)

Two more things sharpen the case. First, at 65 the HSA simply turns into a traditional IRA (a few years later than the real IRA's 59.5, but the same deal), so even a balance you never spend on medical care is still a perfectly good retirement account. Second, unlike an actual traditional IRA, an HSA balance never causes trouble with the pro-rata rule that can complicate a backdoor Roth later (covered in [Post 5](/blog/taxes-are-the-quiet-variable#the-pro-rata-rule)).

So my advice here: **if you can contribute through payroll, max the HSA, or get as close as your budget allows.** (Does this now beat Step 4? On pure tax math, payroll HSA dollars arguably do. I keep the Roth IRA first because its money is never locked behind medical rules and everyone can use it, high-deductible plan or not. If you would rather flip Steps 4 and 5, the math will not punish you.) If payroll contributions are not available to you, direct contributions are still worth making for the income-tax deduction and the tax-free growth; the case is just less lopsided without the FICA edge.

The underused move: treat the HSA as a long-term account, not a checking account for medical bills. The IRS does not impose a time limit on reimbursing yourself, as long as the expense was incurred after the HSA was opened.[^6] So in theory you can pay medical bills out of pocket, save the receipts, let the HSA grow invested for decades, and reimburse yourself years later, tax-free.

I am more cautious on this than some writers, though. The "hold receipts for 30 years" version makes me nervous: you can lose receipts, forget which ones you used, or simply never get around to it, and the optimization is not actually that large compared to just putting the reimbursed money in another tax-advantaged account. Here is the rule I would actually follow. If your HSA balance is large enough to cover a medical bill, reimburse yourself when it is convenient and route that cash into your other retirement accounts, which carry similar tax benefits. Save receipts mainly for the case where your balance is not yet big enough to cover a bill you paid out of pocket. This way you can reimburse yourself once it grows (and protect yourself in the event of an IRS audit, since withdrawals are reported on [Form 1099-SA](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-1099-sa) and you may be asked to prove they were for qualified expenses). And if you do not have an HSA yet and you are eligible, open one as soon as possible, even with a small balance, because the reimbursement clock only starts once the account exists.

A note on keeping those receipts, so this does not become a shoebox of paper: a clear photo or scan is all you need. The IRS accepts digital records, so you can throw away the original receipt as long as the image is legible and stored somewhere durable.[^6] Take a picture when you pay, name the file something like `2026-03-15_dental.pdf`, back it up to the cloud, and forget about it.

On the timing question specifically, since it trips people up. There is no requirement that you reimburse in the same calendar year, and you are not capped by what an expense "should" have cost. The only limits are that the expense happened after the account was open and that any single withdrawal cannot exceed the cash actually in the account at that moment. In numbers:

- You have a $2,000 medical bill in 2026, but only $1,200 sitting in your HSA.
- You can reimburse yourself $1,200 now, the current balance, tax-free.
- You hold the receipt for the remaining $800.
- In 2028, your balance has grown past $800. You reimburse yourself that $800, tax-free, against the same 2026 receipt.

Nothing is lost by waiting. The receipt does not expire, the calendar year does not matter, and you simply collect the rest once the money is there.

A pro tip. When you pay for medical out of pocket, put it on a cashback credit card. (The [Fidelity Rewards Visa](https://www.fidelity.com/spend-save/visa-signature-card) is the one I use, since the unlimited 2% cash back drops straight into a Fidelity account.) You get the credit card cash back, you still keep the receipt, and you reimburse yourself from the HSA whenever you like. The HSA money keeps growing, and you pocket the 2%.

One honest caution to go with the stronger advice. The 401(k) and IRA are walled off until retirement age, and that hard wall quietly does you a favor: you never think of the money as spendable. The HSA has a softer wall. Medical expenses show up every year, so there is always a legitimate reason to reach into it, and a growing balance can start to change how you spend. A hospital sends an outrageous bill and you pay it without question, instead of disputing the charges, because the HSA balance is sitting right there. The monthly massage and pricey chiropractor become "HSA money." That is lifestyle inflation in a medical gown, and it quietly defeats the whole strategy. Treat the HSA as what it really is, a retirement account that also happens to cover true health emergencies, not a healthcare checking account.

The best solution to this is also the best investment: do not need to spend the money. Exercise. Eat well. Sleep enough. Brush your teeth. The healthier you stay, the less you spend, and the more the balance compounds year after year. This is one of the most direct ways the "best investment is you" idea from [Post 1](/blog/money-is-freedom#the-best-investment-is-you) turns into actual dollars saved. Bet on yourself.

A note on the Limited Purpose FSA. If you are on a high-deductible plan with an HSA, you cannot use a regular FSA without breaking your HSA eligibility. But you can pair your HSA with a Limited Purpose FSA, which covers only dental and vision expenses. It exists precisely so HSA holders can get a second slice of pre-tax money for those two categories without disqualifying the HSA. If you have predictable dental or vision spending (an orthodontia bill, new glasses, a planned LASIK procedure), funding a Limited Purpose FSA up to that amount is a clean extra tax break. Just respect the use-it-or-lose-it rule and do not overfund it.

One thing worth knowing is that HSAs cover far more than people assume, including things like sunscreen, therapy, LASIK, and braces. The full list of what qualifies, along with a note about the regular FSA for people who are not on a high-deductible plan, is in [the appendix](#appendix-hsa-eligible-expenses-and-a-note-on-fsas) at the end of this post.

If your employer lets you choose your HSA provider, choose Fidelity. The Fidelity HSA has no fees, full investment options, and automatic high-yield interest on the cash you have not invested. Most employer-default HSAs charge monthly fees and limit you to a small menu of mediocre funds. The fee difference compounds over decades. [Post 4](/blog/the-fidelity-setup#step-5-open-an-hsa-if-you-are-eligible) covers the Fidelity HSA in more detail.

## Step 6: Retirement Accounts Beyond the Match, and Taxable Brokerage

A quick note on accounts before the strategy. I have been saying "401(k)" as shorthand, but the same logic applies to whatever employer retirement account you have. A 403(b) is the version for public schools, hospitals, and nonprofits. A 457(b) is for state and local government employees, and it has a useful quirk: no early-withdrawal penalty once you leave the job, which makes it unusually flexible. The federal government's version is the Thrift Savings Plan (TSP), which has some of the lowest fees anywhere. If you are self-employed, a SEP IRA or a Solo 401(k) lets you contribute far more than a regular IRA, sometimes up to $72,000 in 2026 between the two sides of the contribution.[^4] The mechanics differ, but the priority logic in this post is the same.

This is the step where most personal finance writers tell you to max your 401(k) before opening a taxable brokerage. I do not fully agree with that, and this is why.

The conventional advice prioritizes the 401(k) because of the tax math. With a traditional (pre-tax) 401(k), contributions reduce your taxable income now, the money grows tax-deferred, and you pay tax on withdrawal in (presumably) a lower bracket. With a Roth 401(k), you pay tax now but get the same tax-free growth and tax-free withdrawals as a Roth IRA. Either way the tax advantage is real, especially for high earners in high-tax states.

But the 401(k) has costs that do not show up in the math.

The money is locked up until 59.5. Withdraw early and you pay income tax plus a 10% penalty. There are exceptions (Rule of 55, SEPP withdrawals, hardship withdrawals), but they are narrow. The default is that the money is inaccessible for 25 to 35 years. (After you leave a job, a rollover from your Roth 401(k) to Roth IRA makes your contributions much more accessible.)

You are also limited in what you can invest in. Inside a 401(k), you can usually only buy from a small menu of funds your plan administrator offers. (There are exceptions: some plans offer a brokerage window, and once you leave a job you can roll the 401(k) into an IRA and open up the full menu.) A taxable brokerage, by contrast, lets you buy essentially anything: individual companies, real estate funds, a wider set of index funds. To be precise about why this matters, it is not that the brokerage magically earns more. It is that the money is accessible and flexible. You can liquidate it whenever you want, with no early-withdrawal penalty (you still owe capital gains tax on the profits, but no penalty), and redeploy it into whatever opportunity comes up.

There is tax law risk. Tax laws are not laws of physics. Congress can and does change them. The 401(k) bet assumes the tax treatment that exists today will still exist when you withdraw. That has been a reasonable bet historically, but the timeline is 30 years and the politics are unpredictable. Who knows what the rules look like by then.

What this means in practice: there is a real tradeoff between tax efficiency (401(k) wins) and liquidity and flexibility (taxable brokerage wins). For someone with a high need for flexibility (young, mobile, uncertain career, thinking about starting a business or taking a sabbatical in the next 10 years), splitting between the 401(k) and a taxable brokerage is reasonable, and tilting more toward taxable is defensible. For someone with a stable career and a clear retirement timeline, maxing the 401(k) is the better tax move.

My personal split is to max the Roth IRA every year, contribute some additional 401(k) beyond the match, and put the rest in a taxable brokerage. I value the liquidity, because I value the flexibility it gives me.

For the 401(k) contributions you make beyond the match, you can choose between traditional (pre-tax) and Roth (post-tax) if your plan offers both. The 2026 limit is $24,500 total across traditional and Roth combined for people under 50, and $32,500 total for those 50 and over.[^4] It is one combined limit, not two separate ones. (One 2026 wrinkle for higher earners: if you made more than $150,000 last year, any catch-up contributions now have to go into the Roth side rather than pre-tax.)[^4]

One subtle thing about Roth 401(k) contributions: the percentage you allocate comes off your pre-tax (gross) salary, not your take-home pay. In numbers:

- You make $1,000 in a given paycheck and elect 10% into a Roth 401(k).
- $100 goes into the Roth.
- Your take-home drops by the full $100, even though Roth contributions are "post-tax."

Many people expect the hit to be smaller, because they assume "post-tax" means the contribution is calculated off their after-tax paycheck. It is not; it comes off the gross. (And to answer the natural follow-up: no, you cannot route 100% of your salary into a Roth 401(k), because your paycheck still has payroll and income taxes withheld, and the Roth contribution comes out of the same gross figure those taxes are based on.)

**A note on Roth versus traditional 401(k) and portability.** I contribute to the Roth side of my 401(k), not the traditional side. The reason is portability. When I leave the job, my Roth 401(k) money can roll directly into my Roth IRA, where I get the full menu of funds instead of being stuck with whatever my employer's plan offers. Traditional 401(k) money rolled into a traditional IRA would create pro-rata problems with any future backdoor Roth conversion ([Post 5](/blog/taxes-are-the-quiet-variable#the-pro-rata-rule) covers this).

There is a second payoff to that same rollover, and it is a point that rarely gets written down. Inside a Roth 401(k), an early withdrawal is **pro rata** (the term from [Step 4](#step-4-max-out-your-roth-ira)): every dollar you pull out is treated as a proportional blend of your contributions and your earnings, and the earnings slice gets taxed plus a 10% penalty. A Roth IRA does not work that way. Its withdrawals count as **contributions first**, and contributions come out tax-free and penalty-free at any age. So the moment your old Roth 401(k) lands in your Roth IRA, every dollar you contributed at that job switches from pro-rata money to contributions-first money.[^7] If a slow year ever comes and you genuinely need cash, you can withdraw up to the total you contributed with no tax and no penalty, while all the growth stays behind compounding tax-free. Compare that to holding your flexible money in a taxable brokerage, where dividends and interest are taxed every year and the growth is taxed the day you sell. Once you are 59.5 and the account has been open five years, the question dissolves entirely: everything comes out tax-free, not just the contributions. For someone who wants flexible money and does not expect to stay at one company for decades, Roth 401(k) contributions are far more liquid than they look. To be clear, this is optionality, not a plan: money you pull from a Roth can never be re-contributed, and the compounding you give up is real ([Post 5](/blog/taxes-are-the-quiet-variable#roth-versus-traditional-fully-unpacked) puts numbers on the cost). But the option changes the calculus of how much to send to the Roth side versus the taxable brokerage. [Post 5](/blog/taxes-are-the-quiet-variable#roth-versus-traditional-fully-unpacked) covers the mechanics and the fine print.

**Heads-up on the employer match itself.** Even if you contribute to the Roth side of your 401(k), the employer match itself is almost always made on the traditional (pre-tax) side. That portion of your account is pre-tax money that will be taxed on withdrawal. This is normal and required by most plans. You can convert it to Roth later through an in-plan Roth conversion if your plan allows, but the conversion itself is taxable. Just know the match dollars are not Roth dollars by default.

**When you change jobs**, roll the traditional portion of your old 401(k) into your new employer's 401(k) rather than into a traditional IRA. This keeps the backdoor Roth pathway clean and avoids paying fees on two 401(k) accounts at once. The transfer is tax-free as long as it is done as a direct rollover.

The taxable brokerage has no contribution limit and is the most flexible account you have. You can access the money at any time, for any reason, without penalty (you may still incur taxes, however). That is why the brokerage is where most "freedom money" lives. The Roth and 401(k) are great for retirement, but if you want money to start a business at 38 or take a year off at 45, that money has to live somewhere accessible. The taxable brokerage is the answer.

If your 401(k) plan offers something called the mega backdoor Roth (after-tax contributions plus either in-service withdrawals or in-plan Roth conversions), you can contribute well above the regular limit. Most plans do not offer it. If yours does, [Post 5](/blog/taxes-are-the-quiet-variable#the-mega-backdoor-roth) covers how to use it.

## When Money Is Tight: Sequencing the First Three Steps

Everything above quietly assumed you have surplus left after expenses. Plenty of people do not, at least not yet, and the honest question is what to do when the employer match, the emergency fund, and the high-interest debt are all competing for the same scarce dollar. When one dollar can only go to one place, here is how I would prioritize:

1. **Always take the full employer match first, even while carrying high-interest debt.** A 100% match beats a 21% interest rate, and it is not close. Skipping the match to pay debt faster is one of the biggest mistakes in personal finance.
2. **Build a small starter emergency fund next, something like $1,000 to $2,000.** Just enough that a flat tire or a copay does not send you straight back to the credit card. A thin cushion is what stops you from digging the hole deeper.
3. **Then throw everything else at the high-interest debt until it is gone**, highest rate first. Always.
4. **Only after the high-interest debt is dead** do you grow the emergency fund to its full size and move on to Step 4.

The order is the same order as the rest of the post: match first because it is the highest return available anywhere, a thin cash cushion second because it prevents new high-interest debt, then the debt itself. Being tight on money does not change the priorities. It just shrinks the amounts and makes the sequencing stricter. On the pre-tax-versus-Roth question while you are in this phase: if your 401(k) offers both, lean traditional (pre-tax) for now, since lowering your taxable income frees up a little more cash flow to attack the debt. You can switch to Roth once you are stable.

## A Few More Things Worth Knowing

Live below your means. You cannot build wealth if you do not. None of the priority order above matters if your expenses match or exceed your income.

Automate everything. The 401(k) contribution should come out of your paycheck before you see it. The Roth IRA contribution should be set up as an automatic monthly transfer, or done in one lump sum at the start of each year. The HSA contribution should be automatic. If you never see the money, you cannot spend it, which means lifestyle inflation cannot eat it.

The order is roughly the same regardless of income. A person making $50,000 follows the same priority order as a person making $250,000. The amounts are different. The order is not.

Dollar-cost averaging versus lump sum. Most people end up dollar-cost averaging by default because they invest from each paycheck. This is fine. If you get a windfall (a bonus, an inheritance, a tax refund), the right move is usually to invest it as soon as you can. Statistically, the market goes up in most years, so getting the money in faster wins more often than not.

Set your beneficiaries. Every retirement account, brokerage account, and HSA needs a named beneficiary. This is five minutes per account and saves your family a probate nightmare if something happens to you. Update them whenever life changes (marriage, divorce, kids). This is one of the most common avoidable mistakes in personal finance.

## The Takeaway

Employer match. Emergency fund. High-interest debt. Roth IRA. HSA. The rest split between your retirement account and a brokerage. Roughly in that order.

Each step roughly earns you more than the one after it. People skip steps because they are excited about investing, or impatient with the boring early ones. That impatience is expensive. The reward for getting the order right is that the hardest decisions are mostly behind you. You have built the structure. From here it is mechanical: the right money flows to the right accounts, and compounding takes it from there.

But filling the right accounts is only half the job. An account is just a container. What you put inside it, the actual funds you buy, is what determines whether your money grows at 7% or sits in cash losing to inflation. That is [Post 3](/blog/how-i-pick-what-to-buy): what to actually buy, why index funds win, and the handful of holdings I use across every account above.

## Appendix: HSA-Eligible Expenses and a Note on FSAs

HSAs can be used for more than people realize:

- Sunscreen with SPF 15 or higher
- Contact lens solution and prescription glasses
- Dental work including braces
- Vision care including LASIK
- Mental health therapy
- Period products
- Most over-the-counter medications (allergy meds, pain relievers, cold medicine), since the 2020 CARES Act expanded the list[^8]
- Acupuncture, chiropractic care, and physical therapy (I am not a fan of chiropractic, but it is HSA-eligible if you use it)
- Pregnancy and fertility expenses

The full list is in [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502).[^9] There is also a site called [hsastore.com](https://hsastore.com) that only sells HSA-eligible items, which is useful for figuring out whether something counts. Most HSA providers will give you a debit card you can use directly at the pharmacy, which makes the tax savings as easy as swiping (though I prefer not to use it, because you lose out on the credit card cash back).

A note on the regular FSA. If you do not have an HSA-eligible plan but your employer offers a general-purpose FSA (Flexible Spending Account), it is a more limited version of the same idea. Pre-tax contributions for medical expenses, but with two important catches: the 2026 annual limit is $3,400, and the money is mostly use-it-or-lose-it within the calendar year. You can roll over up to $680 to the following year, but anything above that disappears.[^1] A general-purpose FSA covers the same broad category of medical expenses as an HSA, things like prescriptions, copays, dental, vision, mental health, and OTC meds. (This is different from the Limited Purpose FSA discussed in [Step 5](#step-5-hsa-beyond-the-match), which covers only dental and vision and is designed to pair with an HSA.) If your employer described your FSA as "vision and dental only," that is almost certainly a Limited Purpose FSA, not this one. Check which one you have before you elect, because it changes what you can spend it on. Either way, contribute conservatively given the use-it-or-lose-it rule.
