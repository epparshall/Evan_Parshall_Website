## The Five Things I Look For

When I am deciding whether to buy a fund, I check five things:

1. **Low expense ratio.** Anything under 0.10% is great. Between 0.10% and 0.20% I have to think about why I am paying more.
2. **Mostly qualified dividends.** This determines the tax rate on dividends yielded by the fund.
3. **Broad exposure.** The fund should give me access to a meaningful slice of the market.
4. **Historical growth.** Not last quarter or last year. A decade or more, ideally across at least one downturn, so I can see how the fund behaves in both directions.
5. **Reasonable P/E ratio.** I generally like a price-to-earnings ratio under 30. P/E is one of the best quick signals of whether something is investing versus speculating.

Every term in this checklist gets defined in the next section. The rest of the post is the reasoning behind each item.

## Quick Definitions

A few terms come up constantly. Read this section once and the rest of the post will flow without you having to look things up.

An **index** is a published list of stocks (or bonds) that represents some slice of the market. The S&P 500 is the most famous: a list of about 500 large U.S. companies. Most indexes are weighted by market capitalization, meaning bigger companies get bigger weight in the list.

An **index fund** is a fund that mechanically buys every name in some index, in the proportions the index specifies. The fund manager is not picking stocks. They are matching the list. This is also called passive investing, because the manager makes no active decisions about what to buy or sell.

An **actively managed fund** is the opposite. A human manager picks stocks based on their view of which ones will outperform. They charge more for the work. This is active investing.

An **expense ratio** is the annual fee a fund charges, as a percentage of the money you have invested. A 1% expense ratio on $1,000 means the fund takes $10 per year. On $100,000 at 1% it takes $1,000. The fee is deducted directly from the fund, so you do not see it as a separate charge, but it is real and it compounds.

**Qualified dividends** are dividends from U.S. corporations (and certain qualifying foreign corporations) that you held for at least 60 days. They are taxed at favorable long-term capital gains rates: 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your income. Most readers fall at 15%.[^1]

**Ordinary dividends** are everything else: REIT distributions, dividends from stocks held less than 60 days, interest payments from bonds. They are taxed at your full ordinary income rate, which can reach 37% federally.

**Long-term capital gains** are profits from selling something you held for more than a year. Taxed at the favorable 0/15/20% rates, same as qualified dividends.

**Short-term capital gains** are profits from selling something you held for a year or less. Taxed at full ordinary income rates.

A **mutual fund** is a pool of money invested in a basket of stocks or bonds. You buy shares of the fund, not the underlying stocks. Mutual funds trade once a day, after the market closes, at a price based on what the underlying holdings are worth (the "net asset value"). Mutual funds can be index funds or actively managed.

An **ETF (exchange-traded fund)** is also a pool of money in a basket of holdings, but the shares trade on a stock exchange like an individual stock. You can buy and sell them throughout the day at whatever price the market gives you. ETFs can also be index funds or actively managed, though most are index.

**P/E ratio (price-to-earnings)** is the price of a stock divided by its annual earnings per share. A stock priced at $100 with $5 of earnings per share has a P/E of 20, meaning you are paying $20 for every $1 of current earnings. Funds have a weighted-average P/E based on their holdings, usually listed on the fund's page.

## The Case for Indexing

The argument for indexing was made in 1973 by Burton Malkiel in *A Random Walk Down Wall Street*.[^2] Malkiel was a Princeton economist who argued that stock prices move close enough to random that nobody (not even the smartest professional stock pickers) could reliably beat the market over the long run. The right move for almost every investor, he said, was to buy a fund that owned the whole market at low cost and hold it for decades.

The investment community hated the idea. *A Random Walk Down Wall Street* got brutal reviews. The most famous critique came from professionals who asked, in essence, "why would anyone want guaranteed mediocrity?" Three years later, in 1976, John Bogle launched the first index fund at Vanguard. He hoped to raise $150 million. He raised $11 million. The financial press called it "Bogle's Folly."[^3] Malkiel later joked that he and Bogle might be the only two investors in it. Today, more than half of every dollar invested in U.S. equity mutual funds is in an index fund. The thesis was right. The "guaranteed mediocrity" turned out to beat most active managers, most of the time, by significant margins.

The reframe that matters is this: when you buy an index fund, you are not betting on individual companies. You are betting that the market as a whole, or some country, or some industry, will be larger and more valuable in 30 years than it is today. That is a wildly different question from "will this specific company outperform its peers."

### Most companies fail. The market succeeds anyway.

Hendrik Bessembinder, a finance researcher at Arizona State, ran the numbers on every U.S. stock from 1926 to 2016. He found that the best-performing 4% of listed companies had generated all of the net wealth created by the stock market in that 90-year period.[^4] The other 96% collectively matched the return of one-month Treasury bills. Take that in: more than half of all individual stocks underperformed Treasury bills over their lifetimes, and the median stock had a negative lifetime return.

That is an extraordinary result. The market goes up over time, but most individual companies do not. A small number of huge winners do all the work. If you buy individual stocks, you are betting you can pick the 4%. If you buy the index, you are guaranteed to own them, plus the 96% that drag along. The odds overwhelmingly favor owning everything.

### Malkiel's diversification insight.

Owning one stock carries two risks. The first is market risk: the whole market can go down. The second is company-specific risk: this particular company can have an accounting scandal, a CEO crisis, a product recall, or just get outcompeted by someone else. If you own 500 stocks, the second risk almost completely cancels out. Any single company can blow up; the others soften the blow. The market risk is the only thing left.

The clean trade is this: by indexing, you eliminate company-specific risk without giving up expected return, because the market does not pay you extra for taking on risks you could have avoided through diversification. You only get paid for the risks you cannot avoid. That insight is the entire foundation of modern portfolio theory.

### One more quiet benefit: insider trading.

Insider trading is illegal. It also definitely happens. Executives, board members, and well-connected investors learn things before the public does, and some of them act on it. If you own one stock, you are competing against insiders on that stock. If you own all of the stocks in the S&P 500, the insiders trading any individual company are roughly canceling each other out from your perspective. Indexing does not stop insider trading. It just makes you much less exposed to it.

## Why Active Funds Lose

The case above is the theoretical one. The empirical one is more brutal.

Every year S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes the SPIVA report (S&P Indices Versus Active), which compares actively managed mutual funds to their benchmark index.[^5] They have been doing this for over twenty years. The pattern is consistent.

Over the 15-year period ending December 2024, not a single U.S. equity fund category had a majority of active managers outperform their benchmark index. In the most-watched comparison, large-cap U.S. equity, about 65% of active funds underperformed the S&P 500 in 2024, a figure that climbs above 80% as the time horizon stretches to 10 and 15 years. Fewer than one in six large-cap managers beat the index over 10 years.[^5]

The plain-English version: if you had picked an actively managed large-cap U.S. equity fund at random and held it for 15 years, the odds you would have ended up with less money than a cheap S&P 500 index fund were overwhelming. The longer the time horizon, the worse it gets.

Three reasons actively traded funds are not the move, all worth understanding:

**Fees.** A typical actively managed fund charges 0.5% to 1.2% per year. A typical index fund charges under 0.10%. The active fund has to outperform the benchmark by enough to overcome that fee gap before it even ties the index, every year, forever. Most do not.

**Hidden costs from trading.** Active funds trade more than index funds, often dramatically more. Every trade inside the fund creates costs: short-term capital gains taxes (when the fund sells something it held for less than a year), bid-ask spreads, and execution costs. These do not show up on the expense ratio sheet, but they get passed to you through lower returns. Index funds barely trade; they only rebalance when the index itself changes, which is rare.

**Survivorship bias.** This is the one most people miss. When an actively managed fund underperforms for long enough, the fund family closes it or merges it into a better-performing fund. The poor track record disappears from the marketing materials. The funds you see advertised today are the survivors. SPIVA's methodology tracks what happened to the dead ones, and over a 20-year window nearly 64% of domestic active stock funds were closed or merged.[^5] The graveyard is enormous, and you only get to see the headstones in their brochures.

There is also a strange transparency problem with active funds. The portfolio is usually proprietary. The algorithm or rationale is proprietary. You hand over your money. You do not know what they bought yesterday, you do not know why they bought it, and you pay them more than an index fund charges to know less than the index fund would tell you. That is a strange deal.

### The Medallion exception (and why it does not help you)

The clearest counterexample to "active management cannot beat the market" is Renaissance Technologies' Medallion Fund. From 1988 to 2018, Medallion returned about 66% per year before fees.[^6][^7] It is one of the most extraordinary track records in financial history.

Two things about Medallion are worth noting.

First, the people running it were mathematicians and codebreakers, not finance guys. Jim Simons, the founder, was a former Cold War cryptographer and a world-class differential geometer. He hired physicists, computer scientists, and statisticians. He explicitly avoided people with finance backgrounds. The thing they built does not look like investing; it looks like signal processing applied to price data.

Second, Robert Mercer, one of Medallion's longtime executives, was once quoted explaining how the fund worked: "We're right 50.75% of the time, but we're 100% right 50.75% of the time. You can make billions that way."[^7]

Sit with that. The greatest quantitative trading operation ever assembled, run by mathematicians with one of the largest private computing facilities in the world, refined over three decades, was right about 51% of the time. Just barely better than a coin flip, applied across hundreds of thousands of trades a day. Medallion was closed to outside investors in 1993 and now manages money only for Renaissance employees.

There is a final wrinkle worth saying out loud. Who do you think Medallion was making money off of? Certainly not from index funds, which barely trade. Medallion made money mostly off the individual speculators who thought they could time the market, predict the next move, or pick the next winner. Medallion's edge was, in part, the steady flow of money from people who were sure they knew what would happen next.

The lesson is not that systematic trading is impossible. The lesson is that even the best ever could only squeeze about three-quarters of a percentage point of edge out of the market on each bet, and you should be deeply skeptical of any active manager who thinks they have more.

## Expense Ratios in Dollars

People hear "1%" and think it does not matter much. Run the numbers and it matters enormously. Here is the same lump sum invested at the same gross return, with the only difference being the expense ratio, over 30 years. I use 10% here, the long-term nominal (before-inflation) return of the U.S. stock market:

| Starting Amount | Annual Return (gross) | Expense Ratio | Final Value (30 yr) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $100,000 | 10% | 0.03% (VTI) | $1,730,720 |
| $100,000 | 10% | 0.50% (typical low-cost active fund) | $1,522,031 |
| $100,000 | 10% | 1.00% (typical actively managed fund) | $1,326,768 |
| $100,000 | 10% | 2.00% (advisor-sold fund plus advisor fee) | $1,006,266 |

The difference between the index fund and the typical actively managed fund on the same $100,000 is about $404,000 over 30 years. The difference between the index fund and the "advisor + active fund" version is about $724,000.

Another way to see it. A 1% expense ratio on a 10% gross return is taking 1/10 of your annual return. That is roughly 10% of your gains, every year, forever. People hear "one percent" and shrug. They would not shrug at someone proposing to take 10% of everything they earn.

This is also why I would be skeptical of paying a financial advisor 1% of your assets as an ongoing annual fee, on top of the fund expense ratios. The math is the same. The advisor has to add enough value every year, forever, to outweigh that drag. Most do not.

There is one case where I would relax that. If you are someone who needs constant talking off financial cliffs (panic selling in downturns, chasing the latest hot stock, getting talked into bad products), an advisor who keeps you out of your own way might earn their fee. For most rational people who take the time to learn personal finance, the math is not in the advisor's favor.

A few specific situations where I would still see an advisor:

- **Free.** Fidelity, Vanguard, and Schwab assign a free advisor once your account balance crosses a certain threshold. Free is free. Be wary about them steering you toward high-expense-ratio funds, though. Corporations still have incentives. Make sure they are not nudging you into something with a 0.8% expense ratio when a 0.03% alternative exists.
- **Specific tax situations.** Complicated estate, high-income business, real estate holdings, sudden inheritance. A fee-only advisor or a CPA can save more than they cost on these, but only for specific projects, not for ongoing portfolio management. They are paid for what they know about the tax code, not for picking funds.
- **Specific life events.** Marriage, divorce, death in the family, sudden windfall, business sale. A one-time consultation, paid with a flat fee rather than a percentage of assets, can be worth it.

Otherwise, twenty hours of self-education (or reading all five posts in this series) is worth six or seven figures over a career. The hourly rate on learning personal finance is hard to beat. And honestly, AI is doing a lot of what a financial advisor provides, with no ongoing fee. An LLM that can read your PDFs, spreadsheets, and account positions plus a few hours a year of attention will outperform most paid advisor relationships.

## The Tax Side: Why Stocks Are Tax-Favored

Even at the same gross return, not all asset classes are taxed the same way. Stocks have a meaningful advantage that compounds over decades.

Here is the difference in numbers. You own $10,000 of a fund that pays a 3% dividend per year, or $300 of annual dividend income:

- **Qualified dividend** (most U.S. stock index funds): 15% tax = $45 owed.
- **Ordinary dividend** (REITs, bond interest, foreign dividends from non-treaty countries): 24% tax in the 24% bracket = $72 owed.

That is on one year of dividends from $10,000. Scale it to a $200,000 portfolio across decades and the gap is real money.

Now the same comparison on a $5,000 gain when you sell:

- **Long-term capital gain** (held longer than a year): 15% = $750 owed.
- **Short-term capital gain** (held a year or less): 24% = $1,200 owed.

The difference of $450 came from waiting a few extra months to sell. This is one of the cleanest tax savings available, and it costs nothing but patience.

Important caveat: all of this only matters in a taxable brokerage account. Inside a Roth IRA, 401(k), or HSA, all income and gains are sheltered from tax regardless of type. The qualified-versus-ordinary distinction is irrelevant inside those wrappers. But the taxable brokerage is where most of your accessible "freedom money" probably ends up, so it matters a lot in practice.

Now apply the tax lens to other asset classes you might consider:

**Real estate (direct ownership).** Rental income is taxed as ordinary income at your full bracket. You can deduct depreciation, which is the IRS's allowance for the building wearing out over time; it lets you pretend the building loses a fixed percentage of its value each year on paper, even if it is actually appreciating. The deduction reduces your taxable rental income in the short term. But when you sell, the IRS "recaptures" all the depreciation you claimed and taxes it at up to 25%, plus you owe capital gains on the rest of the appreciation. A 1031 exchange lets you defer this if you reinvest in another property, but you cannot defer forever. The tax math on direct real estate is workable but more complex than stocks and requires more bookkeeping.

**REITs (real estate investment trusts).** A REIT is a company that owns income-producing real estate (apartment buildings, office buildings, shopping centers, data centers) and is required by law to pay out at least 90% of its income to shareholders. The trade-off is that most REIT distributions are taxed as ordinary income at your full bracket, not as qualified dividends. There is a partial offset called the QBI deduction (Qualified Business Income, Section 199A), which lets you deduct 20% of your REIT dividends from your taxable income. So a REIT dividend taxed at the 24% bracket has an effective rate of around 19% after the deduction.[^8] Better than full ordinary rates, but still worse than the 15% on qualified dividends. This is why I would hold any REIT exposure inside my tax-advantaged accounts, not my taxable brokerage.

**Gold and collectibles.** Capital gains on physical gold and other collectibles are taxed at a maximum of 28%, even after holding more than a year.[^1] That is much worse than the 15-20% on stocks. Gold also throws off no income at all: no dividends, no interest, no rent. The only way you make money on gold is when the price goes up. You are giving up the favorable tax rate and the income, in exchange for the diversification benefit of holding a hard asset (that does nothing). Worth it for some people. Not, in my view, worth a large allocation.

**Cryptocurrency.** Taxed as property. Long-term capital gains rates apply if you hold longer than a year (the same favorable 0/15/20% as stocks). But staking yields and rewards are ordinary income at the moment you receive them. Every swap or sale (rotating from one coin to another, paying for something in crypto, moving between wallets in some cases) is a taxable event. The reporting is much messier than stocks because exchanges do not always provide clean cost-basis tracking. More on crypto in the [Speculation section](#speculation-vs-investment).

The cleanest version of all of this: U.S. stocks held more than a year are one of the most tax-favored investments in the U.S. tax code, and broad-market stock index funds deliver almost all of their distributions as qualified dividends. Vanguard publishes the qualified-dividend-income percentage for every one of their funds at investor.vanguard.com.[^9] Their major U.S. equity funds are essentially 100% qualified. Most other fund families publish similar information, but Vanguard makes it the easiest to find.

## ETFs vs Mutual Funds: Where I Put Each

What is the meaningful difference between mutual funds and ETFs?

The structural difference matters more than the trading hours, and it shows up in your tax bill. When you sell shares of a mutual fund, the fund manager has to sell some of the underlying stocks for cash to pay you out. That sale generates capital gains, which get spread across every remaining shareholder at year end. You can owe tax on a mutual fund's distributions even if you personally never sold anything.

ETFs avoid this through a mechanism called in-kind creation and redemption. When large investors want to cash out of an ETF, they swap their ETF shares for an equivalent basket of the underlying stocks rather than for cash. No stocks get sold inside the fund, so no capital gains get realized, so no tax bill gets distributed to you. This is the single biggest reason ETFs are more tax-efficient than mutual funds in a taxable account.

The practical version. You bought $20,000 of a mutual fund five years ago. It has grown to $30,000. You did not sell anything this year. The fund had a busy year of rebalancing and ended up distributing $1,500 of long-term capital gains to its shareholders. You owe about $225 in tax (15% of $1,500). You did nothing and still ended up with a tax bill.

The same $20,000 held in an equivalent ETF: $0 in capital gains distributions, $0 owed, all your gains still compounding inside the fund.

Over decades, in a taxable account, this matters. In a tax-advantaged account (Roth, HSA, 401(k)), it does not. All gains are sheltered regardless.

My rule:

- **Taxable brokerage:** ETFs only. The tax-efficiency of the in-kind structure compounds.
- **Roth IRA and HSA:** passively managed mutual funds are fine. The tax-inefficiency does not reach you.

Inside my Roth I happily buy Fidelity mutual funds ([FSKAX](https://fundresearch.fidelity.com/mutual-funds/summary/315911693), [FSSNX](https://fundresearch.fidelity.com/mutual-funds/summary/316146182), [FTIHX](https://fundresearch.fidelity.com/mutual-funds/summary/31635V638)) because their expense ratios are slightly lower than the equivalent Vanguard ETFs ([VTI](https://digital.fidelity.com/prgw/digital/research/quote/dashboard/summary?symbol=VTI) is the total U.S. market ETF; [VOO](https://digital.fidelity.com/prgw/digital/research/quote/dashboard/summary?symbol=VOO) is the S&P 500 ETF). FSKAX is 0.015% while VTI is 0.03% for the same index. Inside a Roth, the only thing that matters is the expense ratio. I take the cheaper one.

In my taxable brokerage I hold mostly Vanguard ETFs, because the ETF structure keeps the tax drag near zero and Vanguard's expense ratios are extremely competitive.

One asterisk on the Fidelity ZERO funds (FZROX, FNILX, FZILX, FZIPX). Their headline expense ratio is literally 0.00%, which is incredible. They are great inside a Fidelity Roth. The catch is that they cannot be transferred out of Fidelity. They only exist there. Why would you ever want to move brokerages? A few reasons: better tools at another platform, consolidating accounts after a job change, moving away from a brokerage that lost your trust, or just wanting access to a wider menu of funds. If you ever want to move and you hold a ZERO fund in a taxable account, you have to sell first, which triggers capital gains tax. Inside a Roth that is fine because no tax is owed. In a taxable account I would stay with the standard funds (FSKAX, etc.) or Vanguard equivalents to keep optionality.

## Speculation vs Investment

Burton Malkiel made a useful distinction in *Random Walk* that I come back to often.

**Investment** is buying an asset for the income or appreciation you expect it to produce, based on the underlying economics of the business or the inherent value of the asset. You own a piece of a company that makes money and either pays it out as dividends or reinvests it to grow.

**Speculation** is buying an asset because you expect someone else to pay more for it later, regardless of the underlying economics or whether the asset creates any value for the world. You are not betting on the cash flows. You are betting on the next buyer.

Both can make you money. They feel similar from the inside. But they are different activities with different odds.

Many individual investors think they are investing when they are actually speculating. Buying NVIDIA at any price because it keeps going up is speculation, not investment, unless you have actually analyzed the cash flows and think they justify the price. Buying GameStop in early 2021 because Reddit said the squeeze was on was pure speculation. Buying Dogecoin because Elon Musk tweeted about it was speculation. Buying an NFT of a cartoon ape because someone might pay more later was speculation. The people who got out before the crash made money. The people who held on are still down.

A useful tell, when you are trying to figure out which game you are playing: look at the P/E ratio. Different sectors run different P/Es: utilities and energy run low because they grow slowly, tech runs high because investors are paying for future growth. Under 30 is a reasonable general rule of thumb. Over 40 starts to look stretched. If you are buying a fund at a P/E of 25, you are paying for the actual earnings stream of the business (investing). If you are buying a stock at a P/E of 200, you are not really buying earnings, you are buying the bet that someone else will pay even more later (speculation).

There is nothing morally wrong with speculation. Some people make money at it. But it is a different game, played against different opponents (often professionals with information and tools you do not have), and the long-run statistics for individual speculators are not good. It also takes a lot of time to do well, time you could be spending on something that actually matters to you. The financial news media is engineered for speculators, not investors, because there is nothing to talk about every hour of every day if your strategy is "buy index funds and hold them for 30 years." Watching financial news can be a recipe for buying high and selling low.

## A Note on Crypto

I am cautious on crypto, but I do not dismiss it entirely. A small allocation as a diversifier is reasonable for some people. A few rules I would follow:

- **Never put in more than you can afford to lose.** Crypto is not a savings vehicle. It is speculative.
- **Bitcoin only, if you are going to hold anything.** It has the longest track record, the largest network, and a fixed supply (no more than 21 million bitcoin will ever exist). Most other coins have higher inflation than the staking yields they pay you, which means you are losing purchasing power just by holding them. Ethereum is the easy example. You stake it for 3% to 4% a year, the network issues new ETH at a rate that can erode or exceed that, so your "yield" can be negative in real terms. That is not a yield. It is like keeping cash under a mattress that slowly inflates and loses value.
- **Buy once and hold forever.** Transaction fees on most crypto exchanges are brutal, often 1% to 2% per trade. Trading actively destroys the return.
- **Storage matters once the position gets meaningful.** Leaving crypto on an exchange means trusting the exchange not to fail (Mt. Gox, FTX, others). A hardware wallet (cold storage) is a few hundred dollars and removes that risk. Personally, for the small amount I hold, I trust Fidelity's bitcoin product because they are an institution I trust with the rest of my money, and it is built into a familiar platform.

I hold a small amount and have not added in years. It is in the "diversifier" bucket, not the "savings" bucket. It might go up. It might go to zero. If it tanks, I am not ruined.

## One Thing I Pass On: Target-Date Funds

Target-date funds are funds that automatically shift from stocks to bonds as you approach a target retirement year. They are heavily marketed as the no-decision option for retirement accounts. I do not love them, for a few reasons.

First, more management means more fees. A target-date fund's expense ratio is usually higher than the sum of the underlying index funds it holds. You are paying extra for the automatic rebalancing, which you could do yourself in five minutes once a year.

Second, the target year is not always your goal. If you have built enough wealth to live off dividends and growth, you may never want a heavy bond allocation. If you plan to work part-time into your 70s, your target date is later than your nominal retirement age. The fund does not know any of this.

Third, the rebalancing path is opaque. Different fund families use different rules for how aggressively to shift from stocks to bonds. You cannot easily compare them, and the decision is being made for you.

That said, a target-date fund is a much better default than picking individual stocks, and far better than not investing at all. If your 401(k) menu is bad and the target-date fund is the cleanest option there, take it. I just would not use one in an account where I can build my own allocation.

## Real Diversification: Beyond the S&P 500

People who own VOO and call it diversified are missing something. The S&P 500 is market-cap weighted, which means the biggest companies get the biggest weight. Right now the top 10 companies in the S&P 500 are roughly 35-40% of the entire index, and most of those top 10 are large-cap technology companies. So when you own VOO, you are heavily concentrated in U.S. mega-cap tech, even though you think you own "500 companies."

This is not necessarily wrong. Mega-cap tech has been a great place to be. But it is worth knowing what you actually own, and worth thinking about how to balance it.

Some ways to add real diversification:

- **Equal-weight S&P 500 ([GSEW](https://digital.fidelity.com/prgw/digital/research/quote/dashboard/summary?symbol=GSEW), RSP)** gives every company in the index the same weight, regardless of size. This tilts you away from the top 10 concentration. The expense ratio is higher (about 0.09% for GSEW) than VOO's 0.03%, but the diversification benefit can be worth it.
- **Small-cap funds ([VB](https://digital.fidelity.com/prgw/digital/research/quote/dashboard/summary?symbol=VB), FSSNX)** capture U.S. companies too small to make the S&P 500. They tend to behave differently from large caps. Sometimes they outperform, sometimes they lag. Historically they add diversification.
- **Dividend funds ([SCHD](https://digital.fidelity.com/prgw/digital/research/quote/dashboard/summary?symbol=SCHD), [VIG](https://digital.fidelity.com/prgw/digital/research/quote/dashboard/summary?symbol=VIG))** tilt toward profitable, established companies that return cash to shareholders. They tend to be defensive (less volatile) during downturns and pay mostly qualified dividends. SCHD focuses on high-quality dividend payers screened for financial strength. VIG focuses on companies with long histories of growing their dividend year after year.
- **International funds ([VEA](https://digital.fidelity.com/prgw/digital/research/quote/dashboard/summary?symbol=VEA) for developed markets, [VWO](https://digital.fidelity.com/prgw/digital/research/quote/dashboard/summary?symbol=VWO) for emerging markets, FTIHX for total international, [VXUS](https://digital.fidelity.com/prgw/digital/research/quote/dashboard/summary?symbol=VXUS) as the Vanguard total-international ETF).** U.S. stocks have done historically well, but international markets have had decades where they outperformed. Owning some international is a hedge against U.S. underperformance, and against the possibility (worth considering even if you think it is unlikely) that the dollar one day loses its reserve currency status or the U.S. just runs into a long stretch of bad shape.
- **Sector funds ([VGT](https://digital.fidelity.com/prgw/digital/research/quote/dashboard/summary?symbol=VGT), VHT, VFH, VDE, and so on)** let you tilt toward or away from specific industries. I do not buy these to chase performance, and I keep them small, because tilting by sector is a mild form of active management. I use them only to nudge slightly toward sectors that look reasonably priced by P/E when the broader market looks stretched.

A reasonable rule: add diversification when it costs you almost nothing. Adding GSEW at 0.09% to a portfolio of VOO at 0.03% is fine. The expense difference is rounding error compared to the diversification benefit. Adding an actively managed sector fund at 0.80% is not, because the expense ratio eats most of the benefit.

Diversification working in real time: a concrete example. When political tensions flare in places like the Strait of Hormuz (where roughly 20% of the world's seaborne oil passes through), the S&P 500 often sells off. But in my portfolio, holdings like VDE (Vanguard Energy, concentrated in domestic energy companies that benefit directly from higher oil prices) and SCHD (defensive dividend payers that tend to hold up better during volatility) often move in the opposite direction, offsetting part of the loss. That is the whole point of diversification. You are not trying to predict which event will hit. You are setting up a portfolio where no single event takes the whole thing down at once.

## The Takeaway

Low-cost index funds. Mostly qualified dividends. Broad exposure. Reasonable P/E. Hold for the long run.

That is the whole strategy. Everything in this post is a footnote on those five lines. The math is overwhelmingly in your favor and against the alternatives. Active managers cannot consistently beat the market after fees. Expense ratios compound the wrong direction. Real estate and gold do not have the same tax advantages as stocks. Speculation is a different game with worse odds. Crypto might be worth a small allocation as a diversifier, but it is not the savings vehicle people want it to be.

One more thing belongs in this takeaway, because it may decide whether any of the above works for you: at some point while you hold these funds, the market will crash. Not might, it will. In 2008 the S&P 500 fell 57% from its peak and did not see that peak again until 2013. In March 2020 it dropped 34% in five weeks. When it happens, a $200,000 portfolio becomes $120,000 on your screen, every headline says this time is different, and selling will feel like the only responsible thing to do. Here is what history says instead: the loss is only real if you sell it into existence. The people who sold near the 2009 bottom converted a temporary drawdown into a permanent one, and most did not get back in until prices had already recovered past where they sold. The people who kept their automatic purchases running bought the best prices of the decade on schedule, with no courage required in the moment, because the decision had been made years earlier. That is the whole playbook: change nothing, keep buying, and if you have spare cash, buy more, because the same funds are on sale. The investors who do best in a crash are usually the ones who barely look.

You do not have to have a degree in finance to win at this. You have to be patient and avoid the obvious mistakes. Sometimes the right move is to buy something and forget it. There is real research backing this up: a long-running Fidelity study and the famous Barber and Odean paper "Boys Will Be Boys" both found that women's investment accounts tend to outperform men's over long horizons.[^10][^11] The reason is not stock-picking skill. It is that women trade less, hold longer, and panic-sell less often. The boring strategy beats the active one, and the people who do the least often end up with the most.[^12]

The boring portfolio of broad index funds, held for decades, will beat almost every fund manager, almost every advisor, and almost every clever trader. That is not because the boring portfolio is doing anything special. It is because the alternatives are dragging an anchor of fees, taxes, and bad incentives.

## Appendix: A Note on Voting Rights

One thing worth knowing about index funds, since this post has been generally positive about them. When you buy an index fund or ETF, you do not directly own the underlying companies. The fund company does. That means the fund company holds the voting rights on every share, even though you bear the economic exposure.

In practice, the "Big Three" asset managers (BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street) now hold the voting rights to a significant fraction of every large U.S. company through their index funds. Even a 5% stake in a company is enough to swing meaningful votes on board composition, executive compensation, mergers, and shareholder proposals. The Big Three combined hold much more than 5% of most S&P 500 companies.

This is not a reason to avoid index funds. The cost-and-tax math still overwhelmingly favors them. But it is a real tradeoff worth knowing. You are buying broad-market exposure and giving up your individual voice on how those companies are run. If that matters to you, there are some index funds that handle this differently (Vanguard publishes its proxy voting policy, and a few competitors let shareholders pass their votes through). Worth a few minutes of research if it is a concern.
