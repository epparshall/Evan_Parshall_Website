My first real job was in Product Innovation at ZF in Lafayette, Indiana. My boss, Dan Williams, was a big reader, so I asked him for recommendations. He smiled and said *Atlas Shrugged*.

I was confused at first. Why only one book? Then I saw the size of the book.

I don't usually read fiction. I used to think it had less value since it didn't actually happen. But as I was reading this one, I started noticing a lot of parallels to real life. The value isn't in whether it actually happened. It's in whether it makes you think.

The biggest impact for me was how seriously Rand treats individual responsibility. Not in a vague, motivational-poster way, but tied directly to value creation. She frames productive work, building things, solving problems, as a moral act. 

She also pushes a more uncomfortable idea: that people should be judged, and even loved, based on what value they add and not out of obligation or proximity. That one stuck with me more than I expected. After all, who wants to be loved out of obligation?

Most of the book builds outward from there. Rand has a deep frustration with the idea that anyone is entitled to what someone else built or earned. She doesn't treat that entitlement as a minor character flaw, but as a kind of moral corruption. The people in the novel who live off the output of others without contributing, the ones her characters quietly call looters, aren't just free riders. They're the antagonists.

The central thought experiment is hard to ignore. What happens when the people who actually build and run things decide to stop? When competence withdraws? The whole novel rests on the idea that a system can quietly depend on a group of highly productive people, and that most systems never notice until it's too late.

The common criticisms are fair. The characters can feel one-dimensional. The plot strains credibility at points. And the speeches go on way longer than they need to. At times it reads less like a novel and more like a philosophy lecture with a story wrapped around it.

But that's also why it works. The message is impossible to miss, and hard to fully dismiss, even when you disagree with it. This book might be the reason I love Switzerland so much.

I'd definitely say it is worth reading at least once. It genuinely changed how I think.

And yeah. Who is John Galt?
